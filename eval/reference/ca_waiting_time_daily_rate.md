## Baseline: what "a day's wages" is

- **§203(a):** "the wages of the employee shall continue as a penalty from the due date thereof at the same rate until paid or until an action therefor is commenced; but the wages shall not continue for more than 30 days."
- **§200(a):** "'Wages' includes all amounts for labor performed by employees of every description, whether the amount is fixed or ascertained by the standard of time, task, piece, commission basis, or other method of calculation."
- ***Mamika v. Barca* (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 487** (https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2302381/mamika-v-barca/):
  - At 492: "Penalties accrue not only on the days that the employee might have worked, but also on nonworkdays."
  - At 493: "A proper reading of section 203 mandates a penalty equivalent to the employee's daily wages for each day he or she remained unpaid up to a total of 30 days." Also at 493: "Thus, the critical computation required by section 203 is the calculation of a daily wage rate…"
  - At 494, quoting *Nordling v. Johnston* (Or. 1955): where pay is not a fixed daily wage, "it becomes necessary to arrive at the rate per day by computation." The court adds: "Under section 203, an employee's rate of pay must be calculated as a daily figure."
  - The method, at 490: the court took $60,000 ÷ 52 ÷ 40 × 8 = $230.77, where 8 is "the number of hours plaintiff testified he worked each day."
- ***Nishiki v. Danko Meredith, APC* (2018) 25 Cal.App.5th 883, 893** (https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6106757/nishiki-v-danko-meredith-apc/): the penalty is the full daily wage, not the amount underpaid. "Defendant contends that … the proper measure is $80 per day, that is, the amount of the underpayment, rather than the amount of Nishiki's daily wage. We disagree."
- ***Iloff v. LaPaille* (Cal. Ct. App., 1st Dist., Div. 1, Dec. 23, 2025, A163504), certified for publication, slip op. pp. 26–27** (https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/11230791/iloff-v-lapaille/). This is the opinion on remand from *Iloff v. LaPaille* (2025) 18 Cal.5th 551. The official reporter cite was not found. The court held that in-kind rent "should have been incorporated into its calculation of Iloff's daily wages. And as Iloff notes, this daily rate (incorporating rent) could vary depending on how the weekly hours are spread throughout the week." The 2022 opinion said the same thing (*Seviour-Iloff v. LaPaille* (2022) 80 Cal.App.5th 427, https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6800526/).
- **Pin cites.** "Penalties accrue … also on nonworkdays" is at 492 and "A proper reading…" at 493, as `us_final_pay_rules.md` has them. DLSE Manual §4.4 cites *Mamika* as "69 Cal.App.4th"; the correct cite is 68.

## (a) Alternative workweek of 4 × 10 hours: 10 or 8 hours × rate?

**Answer: 10 × the hourly rate, on every calendar day, up to 30.** This is **open**: it is an inference from the method, and no authority squarely addresses an alternative workweek schedule (AWS). Every primary source builds the daily rate from the hours the employee actually works in a day, and under a valid AWS the regular day is 10 straight-time hours.

- **§511(a):** employees may adopt "a regularly scheduled alternative workweek that authorizes work … for no longer than 10 hours per day within a 40-hour workweek without the payment … of an overtime rate of compensation." Wage Order 4-2001 §3(B)(1) uses the same terms. §2(A) defines an AWS as "any regularly scheduled workweek requiring an employee to work more than eight (8) hours in a 24-hour period." So the ninth and tenth hours are regular hours at the straight rate, with no premium.
- **DLSE FAQ, Q6, first example.** The worker "regularly worked 35 hours per week, Monday through Friday … 35 hours/week ÷ 5 days/week = 7 hours/day; 7 hours/day x $8.00/hour = $56.00/day." The method is weekly hours ÷ days worked per week. For an AWS that gives 40 ÷ 4 = 10.
- **Mamika at 490** multiplies by "the number of hours plaintiff testified he worked each day." **Iloff, slip op. p. 27:** the daily rate "could vary depending on how the weekly hours are spread throughout the week."
- **Calendar days are settled.** Mamika at 492 (above). FAQ Q11: "you count all of the calendar days for which the penalty accrues, including weekends, non-workdays (e.g., days off), and holidays." Manual §4.4: "Penalties continue for up to 30 calendar days."

**Conflicts and open points**
- **Kao** (12 Cal.App.5th 947; pin not shown in the CourtListener text) used "his hourly rate of $28.85 multiplied by eight hours of work," with no tie to his schedule. His salary covered 20 hours a week (fn. 5), while the trial court found he worked 50. Mamika and Drumm also default to 8 hours (÷ 40 × 8). A court that reads "8" as the standard day would land on 8 × rate. No authority adopts that reading for an AWS, and none rejects it.
- **The asymmetry.** At the 10-hour figure, the AWS employee's 30-day maximum is 300 hours of pay. A 5 × 8 employee with the same 40-hour week gets 240. No source discusses this. A weekly-based alternative (40 ÷ 5 = 8, or weekly pay ÷ 7 per calendar day) appears in no primary source.
- **An invalid AWS.** If the AWS was not validly adopted, hours 9 and 10 were overtime that was owed. That turns the question into (b) plus a willfulness question under §13520 (below).

## (b) A 5 × 8 schedule with regular overtime (timecards show 9.5 hours most days)

**Answer: regularly scheduled overtime, premium included, goes into the daily rate; occasional overtime does not.** On this fact pattern that gives 8 × rate + 1.5 × (1.5 × rate) = **10.25 × rate**. This is **settled at the agency level and unsettled in the courts**: no California appellate case decides overtime inclusion.

- **DLSE FAQ, introduction:** "In calculating the penalty, overtime wages are considered only if overtime is regularly scheduled each week. Occasional or infrequent overtime is not considered in the calculation of the daily rate of pay for purposes of computing the penalty."
- **FAQ Q7:** "It depends. Regularly scheduled overtime is included in calculating the daily rate of pay for purposes of computing the waiting time penalty. On the other hand, occasional or infrequent overtime is not included…"
- **FAQ Q6, fry cook:** she "regularly worked 45 hours per week, Monday through Friday … 8 hours/day x $10.00 per hour = $80.00; 1 hour/day overtime x $15.00/hour (1½ x $10.00) = $15.00; $80.00 + $15.00 = $95.00 daily rate of pay."
- **FAQ Q6, salesclerk:** he regularly worked 40 hours but worked 4 hours of overtime in his last week, and his rate is "8 hours/day x $10.00/hour = $80.00/day." The FAQ says this "shows that occasional or infrequent overtime is not included."
- **Courts take the broad view of "wages" but have not ruled on overtime.**
  - ***Drumm v. Morningstar, Inc.* (N.D. Cal. 2010) 695 F.Supp.2d 1014** (https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2385945/drumm-v-morningstar-inc/). At 1019–1020: "the waiting time penalty should have been based on Drumm's 'annual wages,' including his commission and bonus." It excluded stock options exercised that year but earned in earlier years, and expense reimbursements (at 1021).
  - **Drumm at 1019** quotes **DLSE O.L. 2003.01.28** (p. 2): "'The wages of the employee' would, of course, include all of the wages – the base rate, the piece rate, the commission and any bonus (Labor Code § 200)."
  - **Iloff** includes in-kind rent, on the strength of §200.
  - Together these support including regular overtime by extension. None of them decides it.

**Ambiguity to flag.** The FAQ's rule says "regularly **scheduled**," but its fry-cook example says "regularly **worked**." A 9.5-hour pattern that appears only on timecards, not on a schedule, falls in that gap. No source resolves it.

## (c) Salaried exempt employee: ÷ 260, ÷ 365, or something else?

**Answer: annual salary ÷ 52 ÷ 5 (that is, ÷ 260) for a five-day week. Every calendar day then counts.** This is **settled in practice**: one published case uses the arithmetic and the agency states it outright. No source endorses ÷ 365.

- **Mamika at 490, 492:** $60,000 ÷ 52 ÷ 40 × 8 = $230.77/day. That is the same as ÷ 260. The court rejected the trial court's "one month's salary."
- **DLSE FAQ Q12:** "one month's salary does not equate to 30-days wages … $30,000.00/year ÷ 52 weeks/year = $576.92/week; $576.92 ÷ 5 days/week = $115.38/day." The FAQ adds: "the maximum penalty will always exceed a person's monthly salary." The FAQ's commission and salary-plus-commission examples use the same ÷ 52 ÷ 5.
- **Drumm at 1019** (jury instruction, approved): "dividing the employee's annual wages by 52 weeks, dividing the resulting figure by 40 hours to obtain an hourly rate, multiplying the hourly rate by eight to obtain a daily wage." At 1021 the court rejected a 43-week divisor. In fn. 5: "$221,500 ÷ 52 ÷ 40 × 8 × 30 = $25,557.69." For a salaried employee with commission or bonus, the annual figure includes them (Drumm; FAQ Q6).
- **÷ 365:** no primary or secondary source found uses it. CourtListener searches for §203 together with "365" found nothing on point. Dividing by 365 would understate the rate: the penalty already runs on calendar days, and the daily rate is a per-workday figure.
- **Open point.** A salaried employee on a four-day week has no authority. On the FAQ's method (weekly ÷ days worked) the divisor would be 208.

## 8 CCR §13520 and the IWC orders

- **§13520:** "a good faith dispute that any wages are due will preclude imposition of waiting time penalties." (a): the defense must be one "which, if successful, would preclude any recovery on the part of the employee." Defenses "unsupported by any evidence, are unreasonable, or are presented in bad faith" do not qualify.
- **Manual §4.2.1:** "an employer cannot withhold all of the wages due an employee based on a purported good faith dispute as to a portion of those wages."
- §13520 governs **whether** a penalty applies, not the daily rate. It bites on the facts here in three ways:
  1. **An invalid AWS or unpaid regular overtime.** Under *Kao*, a good-faith dispute over the disputed overtime "precludes an award of waiting time penalties" on it, and "Waiting time penalties are properly limited to the uncontested wages due at the time of Kao's termination." Kao still assessed the penalty for the undisputed late wages, at the full daily rate.
  2. **§227.3 "final rate" (question d).** *Mills* (below) found no willfulness, because the law was unsettled.
  3. **The penalty is the full daily wage, not the disputed amount** (Nishiki at 893; Drumm fn. 2, which rejects the view that "only the withheld wages continue as the waiting time penalty").
- **The IWC orders** (Wage Order 4-2001 read) define AWS and overtime. They say nothing about how the §203 rate is computed.

## (d) A combined PTO bank paid out under §227.3: "final rate" including a regular shift differential?

**Answer: split. No California appellate court has decided it.**

**That the bank counts as vacation is settled.**
- **Manual §15.1.12:** "leave time which is provided without condition is presumed to be vacation no matter what name is given to the leave by the employer."
- **Manual §51.6.20, footnote:** "So-called 'Paid Time Off' (PTO) programs sometimes lump all time off together … leave time which is provided without condition is presumed to be vacation."
- **O.L. 1990.09.24:** a PTO bank lumping vacation, holidays, sick leave and personal days was treated "under the vacation rules."

**The rate is the split.**
- ***Mills v. Target Corp.* (9th Cir. Mar. 6, 2023, Nos. 21-56308/21-56309), unpublished memorandum, "not precedent except as provided by Ninth Circuit Rule 36-3"** (https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/memoranda/2023/03/06/21-56308.pdf).
  - At 3: "Because California courts have not answered this question…"
  - At 3: "We read 'final rate' to refer to final 'wage' rate"
  - At 5: final rate means "the 'final rate of wage pay,' which in this case includes Mills's base pay and the pay differentials she received when she was terminated." The differential was $2/hour on a $13 base.
  - At 5–6: no §203 penalty, because "the law on this issue has been uncertain, as multiple courts have reached opposing conclusions."
- **Contra: *Drumm* at 1019–1020 (federal district court).** "An employer must, in other words, pay out vested vacation time at the rate at which the employee would have been paid during a vacation, had he taken one." Base-only vacation pay was "proper."
- **Contra: DLSE O.L. 2003.01.28 at 2–3.** §227.3 "does not dictate the terms of the policy respecting the amount paid." Its worked example is a shift differential: an employer may prospectively drop the differential from vacation pay, "but the vacation wages accrued by those employees which included the shift differential is accrued at that calculated amount and must be paid based on that calculation."
- **On Mills's side: *Wang v. Chinese Daily News* (C.D. Cal. 2006) 435 F.Supp.2d 1042, 1048–1049.** Vested days must be bought back "at the employees' actual daily wage" and "computed by reference to the employee's regular rate of pay." That was a flat $64/day buy-back, not a differential.
- ***Bell v. H.F. Cox, Inc.* (2012) 209 Cal.App.4th 62, 75** allows a vacation rate below the regular rate for **current** employees. It expressly sets aside "the situation where an employee is terminated with unused vacation time," so it does not decide the termination rate.
- **Secondary-source flag.** Seyfarth, *Cal-Peculiarities 2024* §7.19.1 (p. 250, https://content.seyfarth.com/publications/Cal-Peculiarities-2024/250; read in the earlier session, see `us_final_pay_secondary.md`) states Mills's rule and labels it unpublished. It omits the contrary DLSE letter and Drumm. HR Dive and the Winston alert are **UNREAD**.

## (e) Does a floating holiday count as vacation under §227.3?

**Answer: on the DLSE's view it is vacation if it can be taken at the employee's discretion, and not vacation if it is tied to a specific event or date.** This is **settled at the agency level**, with consistent dicta from a published appellate case. Two federal courts declined to decide it.

- **O.L. 1986.10.28:** "a. Floating holidays and b. general absences would be considered vacation time under the Suastez decision."
- **O.L. 1992.04.27 at 3** (birthday and anniversary "floating holidays"): "if an employer offers 'time off' which is not contingent upon the happening of a specific event (i.e., sickness, a holiday, bereavement, etc.) the time off will be treated as a vacation." If the holiday is limited to the date of the event, "the additional holidays would not be considered vacation". If it "need not be taken on the day of the event … then the 'holidays' are 'personal' holidays and are subject to the Suastez principles." Fn. 4: DLSE "will accept a program which would allow the employee to take the 'anniversary' or 'birthday' holiday any time within a week from the date it arises."
- **Manual §15.1.12:** the leave must have "an objective standard by which it can be established that the leave time is attributable to holidays … Tying the right to take the time to a specific event … would suffice."
- ***Paton v. Advanced Micro Devices, Inc.* (2011) 197 Cal.App.4th 1505, 1519** (https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5665913/): "some employers give paid time off for state or federal holidays. The right to this type of time off does not vest with day-to-day employment; it vests upon the occurrence of the holiday." Vacation "is not conditioned upon the occurrence of any event or condition." This is dicta in a sabbatical case.
- **Courts that avoided the question:**
  - ***Gunther v. IBM* (9th Cir. Mar. 20, 2019, No. 17-56626), unpublished** (https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/memoranda/2019/03/20/17-56626.pdf), at 2: "It is therefore immaterial whether PCH days should be treated as vacation days for purposes of section 227.3." PCH means personal-choice holiday.
  - ***Reznik v. IBM* (N.D. Cal. June 7, 2016, No. 15-cv-02419-YGR), Dkt. 56 at 6–7** (https://cases.justia.com/federal/district-courts/california/candce/4:2015cv02419/287997/56/0.pdf): "the Court need not reach the merits of IBM's arguments that PCH days are not required to be treated as vacation days."
- **No California appellate holding on floating holidays was found.**
