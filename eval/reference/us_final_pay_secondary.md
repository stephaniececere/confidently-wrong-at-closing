# U.S. final pay: secondary-source cross-check

Companion to `us_final_pay_rules.md` (the primary-text file, kept separately). This file tests the answer key's expected rules against secondary sources (law-firm alerts, CalChamber, DLSE manual and FAQs, agency guidance) and against the cases those sources rely on, where the case was reachable. Every quote below was read in the source at the URL given. **UNREAD** marks anything not read in full. Checked November 1, 2026.

## Verdicts at a glance

| # | Rule | Verdict | The qualification that matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | CA discharge: final wages due immediately; §203 penalty up to 30 days if willful | **QUALIFIED** | The 30 days are **calendar** days (Mamika). Seyfarth's treatise misstates this as "30 workdays." A good-faith dispute bars penalties. A temporary layoff with no return date triggers §201. "Final rate" includes more than base pay (Mills, unpublished). |
| 2 | CA vacation vests, no forfeiture; caps OK; PTO = vacation; sick leave not paid out | **CONSISTENT** | Leave given "without condition" counts as vacation whatever it's called, so a designated sick bank has to be genuinely tied to sickness. The DLSE also polices the size of a cap. |
| 3 | CA §227.3 CBA "otherwise provides" | **CONSISTENT, sharpened** | A waiver must be "clear and unmistakable": it must "mention either the statutory protection being waived or, at a minimum, the statute itself." A CBA that only addresses vacation at termination doesn't qualify. If the waiver is met, Suastez does not apply and forfeiture can stand. §203 penalties can't be waived (§219). |
| 4 | CA exempt salary 2× state MW; >50% duties; §514 | **CONSISTENT** | The 2026 figures are $16.90 and $70,304. **Most important finding:** two of the cited authorities don't say what the key assumes. (a) On NY, Glenville Gage is a forfeiture **win for the employer**. The Appellate Division (aff'd 52 N.Y.2d 777, 1980) enforced a posted forfeiture rule that employees knew from custom, and it held that §191 timing does not govern vacation pay. The statute's notice requirement can be met by posting. So a key that reads "written notice to the employee is required for forfeiture" is too strict, and one that cites Glenville Gage for "vacation must be paid" cites it backwards. (b) On CA §203, the penalty runs 30 **calendar** days, nonworkdays included (Mamika). A widely used secondary source (Seyfarth, *Cal-Peculiarities* 2024) gets this backwards, so a model that cites it can be confidently wrong.

## 1. California: final pay on discharge and §203 waiting-time penalties — QUALIFIED

The core rule holds. The qualifications concern how the penalty is computed, the willfulness defense, and what counts as a discharge.

**30 calendar days, not workdays.** *Mamika v. Barca* (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 487, 492–93, read on CourtListener (https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2302381/mamika-v-barca/):
> "Under this scheme, unpaid wages continue to accrue on a *daily* basis for up to a 30-day period. Penalties accrue not only on the days that the employee might have worked, but also on nonworkdays."
> "A proper reading of section 203 mandates a penalty equivalent to the employee's daily wages for each day he or she remained unpaid up to a total of 30 days."
> "Thus, the critical computation required by section 203 is the calculation of a daily wage rate, which can then be multiplied by the number of days of nonpayment, up to 30 days."

**Secondary-source error.** Seyfarth, *Cal-Peculiarities 2024*, p. 303, fn. 154 (https://content.seyfarth.com/publications/Cal-Peculiarities-2024/303), raw page text:
> "See Mamika v. Barca, 68 Cal. App. 4th 487, 492-93 (1998) (penalty provided for in section 203 is 30 workdays, not merely 30 calendar days)."

The opinion says the opposite: penalties accrue on nonworkdays, and "days" means calendar days. The key should say a day's wages for each **calendar** day, up to 30.

**Verified in primary text.** The §203 text (read on leginfo) is silent on the kind of day: "the wages of the employee shall continue as a penalty from the due date thereof at the same rate until paid or until an action therefor is commenced; but the wages shall not continue for more than 30 days." *Mamika* (Third District, published) construes that silence: the penalty accrues on "nonworkdays" too, citing *Iverson v. Superior Court* (1985) 167 Cal.App.3d 544, 548 ("unless otherwise specified, 'days' means 'calendar days'"). It rests on the statute's purpose: "The targeted wrong is the delay in payment, a wrong that continues so long as payment is not made." Seyfarth's fn. 154 (re-read on its own page) cites *Mamika* for the reverse, so it's a mis-citation, not a competing authority. A CourtListener search of California and federal opinions for "30 workdays," "thirty workdays," "30 working days" or "thirty working days" with §203 returns nothing. Calendar days is settled.

**Willfulness and good-faith dispute.** The same Seyfarth page, fn. 155, quotes 8 CCR §13520 via *Smith v. Rae-Venter Law Grp.* (2002) 29 Cal.4th 345, 354: "a good faith dispute that any wages are due will preclude imposition of waiting time penalties under Section 203". Fn. 158 (*Diaz v. Grill Concepts*): "Labor Code section 203 does not imbue trial courts with the discretion to waive or reduce waiting time penalties." *Choate* (below) is the vacation example: no penalty, because the waiver standard was unsettled until that case.

**Temporary layoff.** ArentFox Schiff (Lynd, Rafuse, Weston), "In California, Temporary Layoff Requires Immediate Payout of Vacation," JD Supra, Oct. 12, 2023 (https://jdsupra.com/legalnews/in-california-temporary-layoff-requires-9245110), on *Harstein v. Hyatt Corp.* (9th Cir.): "employers must timely pay unused vacation and PTO at the time of a temporary layoff." The case text and its date are **UNREAD**.

**"Final rate."** Seyfarth, *Cal-Peculiarities 2024*, §7.19.1 (https://content.seyfarth.com/publications/Cal-Peculiarities-2024/250): "According to an unpublished Ninth Circuit ruling in Mills v. Target Corporation, 'the "final rate" means the employee's "final wage rate," as opposed to the final base hourly rate.'" *Mills* itself is **UNREAD**. The Winston alert redirected and could not be read.

**Statutory carve-outs.** Lab. Code §201(a) (read on leginfo) gives seasonal perishable-food layoffs up to 72 hours. Seyfarth p. 303 also notes the temporary-services rules (§201.3) and motion picture (§201.5). None of these touch an ordinary discharge.

**2025–2026 changes:** none found. A search for 2026 California legislation or appellate decisions on final pay, vacation payout or §203 turned up nothing new. The 2026-dated guides restate the existing statutes.

## 2. California: vacation vesting, caps, PTO, sick leave — CONSISTENT

DLSE *Policies and Interpretations Manual* §15 (pages dated March 2006; cover says revised August 2019), https://www.dir.ca.gov/dlse/dlsemanual/dlse_enfcmanual.pdf:
> §15.1.1: "Because such vacation entitlements constitute deferred wages which vest as they are earned, any entitlement to vacation is a proportionate right and vests as labor is rendered. ... Vacation pay may not be forfeited for failure to take the vacation under a so-called 'use it or lose it' policy. (Boothby v. Atlas Mechanical (1992) 6 Cal.App.4th 1595, 1601.)"
> §15.1.4: "Vacation plans may not have a 'use it or lose it' provision as such provision would be an illegal forfeiture. However, a variant of a 'use it or lose it' policy whereby a cap is placed on the amount of vacation which accrues if not taken is acceptable. (Henry v. Amrol (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d Supp. 1 ...)"
> §15.1.12: "The DLSE has always opined that leave time which is provided without condition is presumed to be vacation no matter what name is given to the leave by the employer. ... there must be an objective standard by which it can be established that the leave time is attributable to holidays, sick leave, bereavement leave or other specified leave."
> §15.1.3: probation periods during which no vacation vests "are permitted."

Seyfarth, *Cal-Peculiarities 2024*, §7.19 (p. 250): "'use it or lose it' policies are unenforceable, and 'paid time off' is treated as vacation." p. 251 (https://content.seyfarth.com/publications/Cal-Peculiarities-2024/251), as summarized by the fetch tool: the cap must be reasonable, and the DLSE withdrew an opinion letter that set it at 1.75× the annual accrual. That summary is not verbatim.

**Sick leave.** DLSE Paid Sick Leave FAQ (updated Dec. 31, 2025), https://www.dir.ca.gov/dlse/paid_sick_leave.htm, on payout at separation: "No, not unless your employer's policy provides for a payout." On PTO plans, the sick leave law "does not address in any way, nor impact, how employers must compensate employees under existing paid time off plans," and the FAQ points to §227.3 for vacation at termination. On rehire within 12 months, accrued sick leave must be restored, but an employer is "not required to restore previously accrued and unused paid time off (PTO)" that was paid out.

**Nuance for the key:** a "separately designated" sick bank is safe from payout only if it really is conditioned on sickness. Per §15.1.12.1 (O.L. 1987.03.11), sick-leave hours usable for "personal compelling business" above a threshold were treated as vested vacation at termination.

## 3. California: the §227.3 CBA exception — CONSISTENT, with the test made concrete

*Choate v. Celite Corp.* (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 1460, read on CourtListener (https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1037225/choate-v-celite-corp/):
> "To be clear and unmistakable, a waiver must do more than speak in '"[b]road, general language."' ... It must be specific, and mention either the statutory protection being waived or, at a minimum, the statute itself."
> "The Agreements here neither mention pro rata vacation pay nor cite section 227.3. Celite points out that the Agreements 'affirmatively address' vacation payments upon termination. But discussing a topic while at the same time saying nothing about the statutory right at issue does not effect a clear and unmistakable waiver of that right."
> On Livadas: "section 227.3's plain language empowers a union to waive its protections whereas section 219 prohibits waiving the protections of sections 201 and 203."
> Disposition: reversed, "ordered to grant summary adjudication to Celite on the waiting time penalties count." The failure to pay was not willful because "this is the first case to define the standard for waiver under section 227.3."

DLSE Manual §15.1.6: "the provisions of the Suastez case do not apply where the opt-out is met and DLSE would not have jurisdiction to determine whether vacation pay is due. In Choate ... the court held the union collective bargaining agreement must contain a provision explicitly waiving the anti-forfeiture protections set forth in Labor Code section 227.3." The DLSE keeps jurisdiction over waiting-time penalties after any CBA arbitration.

Seyfarth p. 250: "The Court of Appeal has interpreted this exemption narrowly", and the test is whether the CBA "clearly and unmistakably waives" §227.3 rights.

**For the key:** yes, a CBA can permit forfeiture of accrued vacation at termination, but only by expressly naming §227.3 or its no-forfeiture protection. Past practice, implied waiver, or a clause that only addresses vacation at termination is not enough. The §201/§203 timing and penalty rights can't be waived by the CBA at all.

## 4. California: exempt status and §514 — CONSISTENT

**2026 figures.** DIR News Release 2025-118 (Dec. 5, 2025), https://www.dir.ca.gov/DIRNews/2025/2025-118.html: "the state minimum wage will increase from $16.50 to $16.90 per hour on January 1, 2026" and "an employee must earn no less than twice the state minimum wage for full-time employment ... $16.90 × 2 × 40 hours/week × 52 weeks/year = $70,304." Davis Wright Tremaine (Rodriguez, Capell), Dec. 15, 2025 (https://www.dwt.com/blogs/employment-labor-and-benefits/2025/12/california-minimum-wage-state-and-local-increases): "exempt employees will need to earn an annual salary of at least $70,304."

**Local MW does not raise the threshold.** CalChamber (Vanessa M. Greene), Dec. 2, 2025 (https://hrcalifornia.calchamber.com/articles/california-local-minimum-wage-rates-increases-starting-january-1-2026): "the California state minimum wage rate — not the local minimum wage rate —determines the salary threshold".

**Industry exception (qualifies "2× state MW" for health care).** DLSE Health Care Worker Minimum Wage FAQ (updated June 2025), https://www.dir.ca.gov/dlse/Health-Care-Worker-Minimum-Wage-FAQ.htm: covered exempt employees "must receive a salary of at least 1.5 times the new health care worker minimum wage or two times the state minimum wage", "whichever is greater." The FAQ's rate table runs through 6/30/26 ($24 for large systems and large-county facilities). The post-July 1, 2026 step is **UNREAD** in a primary or official source. Fast food is reported to differ but is **UNREAD**.

**Computer professionals.** Thompson Coburn (Michael S. Kun), Nov. 3, 2025: "has set the 2026 threshold for computer professionals at $122,573.13 annually."

**Duties.** DLSE Manual §51.2: "The term 'Primarily Engaged In' means that more than one-half (½) of the employee's work time must be spent engaged in exempt work and differs substantially from the federal test which simply requires that the 'primary duty' of the employee fall within the exempt duties."

**§514.** Seyfarth, *Cal-Peculiarities 2024*, p. 221 (https://content.seyfarth.com/publications/Cal-Peculiarities-2024/221) quotes the conditions: the agreement must "expressly provide[] for the wages, hours of work, and working conditions," "provide[] premium wage rates for all overtime hours worked," and give "a regular hourly rate of pay for those employees of not less than 30 percent more than the state minimum wage." It adds: "for purposes of this exemption ... the governing definition of 'overtime' is provided by the CBA, not the Labor Code" (that's *Vranish v. Exxon Mobil*). Seyfarth flags as unsettled whether **every** unit member must earn 130%, and suggests the exemption likely survives a non-suing member who falls short. 2026 arithmetic (our calculation, not quoted): $16.90 × 1.3 = $21.97.

## 6. New York: vacation forfeiture and 2026 thresholds — QUALIFIED

**Glenville Gage enforced the forfeiture.** *Matter of Glenville Gage Co. v. Industrial Bd. of Appeals*, 70 A.D.2d 283 (3d Dept 1979), read on CourtListener (https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5833868/glenville-gage-co-v-industrial-board-of-appeals/), aff'd for the reasons stated, 52 N.Y.2d 777 (1980) (https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5533643/glenville-gage-co-v-industrial-board-of-appeals/). The posted notice read: "ALL EMPLOYEES WHO ARE TERMINATED OR QUIT BEFORE VACATION PERIODS FORFEIT THEIR VACATION PAY." The court held:
> "All that is required by section 198-c is that an employer abide by the terms of his agreement to provide benefits. Further, there is no substantial evidence in this record which could support a finding that the employees were not aware of the vacation pay policy of the employer regardless of their precise knowledge of an agreement at the time they commenced their employment."
> "It, therefore, appears it was the legislative intent that 'vacation pay' was not included in 'wages' for the purpose of the mandatory provisions of section 191 as to the time of payment of wages upon the termination of employment."

The board's order to pay was annulled. The employees who testified denied seeing the notice, but they knew the custom, and that was enough. The court distinguished *De Vita v Rand McNally*, where the rules "were not told to him nor were they posted."

**Notice can be by posting.** Lab. Law §195(5) (statute text as downloaded): the employer must "notify his employees in writing or by publicly posting the employer's policy on sick leave, vacation, personal leave, holidays and hours." Jackson Lewis (Noel P. Tripp), Nat'l L. Rev., May 29, 2014 (https://natlawreview.com/article/new-york-state-appellate-court-reinforces-employers-right-to-define-terms-vacation-p), on *Linwood v. United Activities Unlimited*, 43 Misc.3d 131(A) (App. Term 2014): the plaintiff "failed to prove that she had a contractual entitlement to be paid for vacation days" beyond pro rata. The procedure manual satisfied §195(5), and as a supervisor earning over $900/wk she fell outside §198-c.

**NY DOL wording (via a mirror).** LawHelpNY's mirror of the NY DOL Wages and Hours FAQ, last reviewed Sept. 25, 2023 (https://lawhelpny.org/resource/wages-and-hours-frequently-asked-questions-wh): "The rules for paying unused vacation time also depend on the employer's written policy about vacation and/or resigning." "IF: an employee has earned vacation time AND: there is no written forfeit policy THEN: the employer must pay the employee for the accrued vacation." The dol.ny.gov original is **UNREAD**.

**Executive exclusion.** Lab. Law §198-c(3), current text: "This section shall not apply to any person in a bona fide executive, administrative, or professional capacity whose earnings are in excess of one thousand three hundred dollars a week." Older secondary sources give $900 (Pospis Law, Oct. 28, 2013, https://pospislaw.com/?p=5326; the Jackson Lewis piece above). For excluded employees, vacation is a contract claim. Pospis quotes *Litras*: "vacation and sick pay are purely matters of contract between an employer and employee."

**For the key:** the default (no forfeiture policy, so earned vacation is payable) holds and matches the DOL's stated position. But a forfeiture can be enforced on a **posted** policy or a known practice (Glenville Gage). Where the courts and the DOL differ, the DOL's "written forfeit policy" is the stricter reading. Glenville Gage supports the employer, not the employee.

**2026 thresholds (executive/administrative).** SHRM (Cartafalsa, Zagger, Ogletree Deakins), Feb. 21, 2024 (https://shrm.org/topics-tools/employment-law-compliance/ny-employers-salary-thresholds): "$1,275 per week ($66,300 per year) on Jan. 1, 2026" for NYC, Nassau, Suffolk and Westchester; "$1,199.10 per week ($62,353.20 per year) on Jan. 1, 2026" for the rest of the state. The professional exemption has no state salary test ("The new NYDOL regulations do not change those rules"). Hodgson Russ (Dec. 22, 2023, corrected) gives the same two figures. Fisher Phillips (May 18, 2023) projected $1,200 upstate, a rounded pre-final figure, so **$1,199.10 is the adopted number**. One search result's "$1,275.50" is an outlier. The 2026 MW is $17.00 downstate and $16.00 upstate. **2026 note:** the NY DOL page (https://dol.ny.gov/minimum-wage-0) says "On January 1, 2027, New York's minimum wage will stay the same." No CPI step was applied for 2027, which matters only if a task runs into 2027.

## 7. Texas — CONSISTENT

**Rule text.** 40 TAC §821.25, TWC rules PDF (https://www.twc.texas.gov/sites/default/files/ogc/docs/rules-chapter-821-payday-twc.pdf; amended eff. Sept. 20, 2010):
> (a) "vacation pay and sick leave pay are payable to an employee upon separation from employment only if a written agreement with the employer or a written policy of the employer specifically provides for payment."
> (g) "paid time off (PTO) or paid days off (PDO) are wages unless the employer's written policy defines PTO or PDO as something other than a combination of vacation pay, holiday pay, sick leave pay, parental leave pay, or severance pay. PTO or PDO is payable to an employee upon separation from employment only if a written agreement with the employer or a written policy of the employer specifically provides for payment."
> (c) The sale of a business "is a termination of employment with all of the employer's employees."

**TWC "Especially for Texas Employers," Accrued Leave Payouts** (https://efte.twc.texas.gov/accrued_leave_payouts.html): payout is "required under the Texas Payday Law only if such a payment is promised by the employer in a written policy or agreement"; "If no such policy exists, the company would not owe such a payment." TWC's sample policy is a model of a lawful forfeiture: "Unused paid leave is forfeited when an employee separates from employment," with exceptions for economic layoffs and resignations "with at least two weeks' advance written notice."

**Timing.** TWC Final Pay page (https://efte.twc.texas.gov/final_pay.html): "the final pay is due within six (6) calendar days of discharge"; for a resignation, "the next regularly-scheduled payday." Final pay includes "fringe benefits payable under a written policy," with the same deadline "unless a different payout schedule is provided in the wage agreement or policy." Employer-initiated and mutual-agreement separations are "generally regarded as involuntary."

## 8. Federal FLSA salary level — CONSISTENT

DOL final rule, technical amendment, Federal Register public-inspection doc. 2026-09839 (https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-09839.pdf; read via pdftotext):
> Both vacatur orders "remain final judgments following the dismissal of the appeal in each case by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit on May 5 and 7, 2026, respectively." The orders are *Texas v. DOL* (E.D. Tex. 2024) and *Flint Avenue* (N.D. Tex. Dec. 30, 2024).
> "the operative version of the Department's part 541 regulations is the version of these regulations that was in place on June 30, 2024, ... and which the Department has been enforcing."
> Restored text: "not less than $684 per week"; HCE "at least $107,432."
> Effective "immediately upon publication."
> Fn. 14: the amendment "does not foreclose the Department from engaging in notice and comment rulemaking in the future to update the part 541 regulations."

Proskauer (Allan Bloom), Law and the Workplace, May 14, 2026 (https://www.lawandtheworkplace.com/2026/05/dol-makes-it-official-2024-overtime-rule-is-gone/): "the federal salary threshold for most EAP exemptions is back to $684 per week"; "the HCE threshold is $107,432 annually"; publication "scheduled for ... May 15, 2026." **2026 change:** the regulation now matches what was already being enforced. The dollar figure did not change.
