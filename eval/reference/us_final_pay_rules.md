## 1. California: final pay on involuntary termination and the waiting-time penalty

**Answer.** On discharge all earned wages, including vested vacation, are due immediately (§201). A willful failure to pay continues the employee's wages as a penalty "at the same rate" for each calendar day until paid, capped at 30 days (§203). A payment made N days late costs N × the daily wage, up to 30 days. The daily wage is the employee's daily rate of pay (Mamika). No primary authority read settles whether overtime or commissions go into that daily rate.

**§201(a)**, timing on discharge:

> "If an employer discharges an employee, the wages earned and unpaid at the time of discharge are due and payable immediately."

**§202(a)**, resignation, for contrast:

> "If an employee not having a written contract for a definite period quits his or her employment, his or her wages shall become due and payable not later than 72 hours thereafter, unless the employee has given 72 hours previous notice of his or her intention to quit, in which case the employee is entitled to his or her wages at the time of quitting."

**§203(a)**, the penalty (as amended by Stats. 2019, ch. 700, eff. Jan. 1, 2020):

> "If an employer willfully fails to pay, without abatement or reduction, in accordance with Sections 201, 201.3, 201.5, 201.6, 201.8, 201.9, 202, and 205.5, any wages of an employee who is discharged or who quits, the wages of the employee shall continue as a penalty from the due date thereof at the same rate until paid or until an action therefor is commenced; but the wages shall not continue for more than 30 days."

**§200(a)** defines "wages" to include commissions:

> "'Wages' includes all amounts for labor performed by employees of every description, whether the amount is fixed or ascertained by the standard of time, task, piece, commission basis, or other method of calculation."

**"Willful": 8 CCR § 13520** (official CCR text, read in the LII reproduction):

> "A willful failure to pay wages within the meaning of Labor Code Section 203 occurs when an employer intentionally fails to pay wages to an employee when those wages are due. However, a good faith dispute that any wages are due will preclude imposition of waiting time penalties under Section 203."

> "(a) Good Faith Dispute. A 'good faith dispute' that any wages are due occurs when an employer presents a defense, based in law or fact which, if successful, would preclude any recovery on the part of the employee. … Defenses presented which, under all the circumstances, are unsupported by any evidence, are unreasonable, or are presented in bad faith, will preclude a finding of a 'good faith dispute.'"

**How the penalty is computed: *Mamika v. Barca* (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 487.** The days are calendar days, and the measure is the daily wage. At 492:

> "Under this scheme, unpaid wages continue to accrue on a *daily* basis for up to a 30-day period. Penalties accrue not only on the days that the employee might have worked, but also on nonworkdays."

At 493:

> "A proper reading of section 203 mandates a penalty equivalent to the employee's daily wages for each day he or she remained unpaid up to a total of 30 days."

> "Thus, the critical computation required by section 203 is the calculation of a daily wage rate, which can then be multiplied by the number of days of nonpayment, up to 30 days."

At 494, the holding:

> "Under section 203, an employee's rate of pay must be calculated as a daily figure, which can then be multiplied by the number of days of nonpayment for a maximum of 30 days."

The salaried employee's daily rate in Mamika (at 490) is the annual salary ÷ 52 ÷ 40 × 8 hours: $60,000 → $230.77/day → $6,923.10 for 30 days. The court rejected the trial court's cap of "one month's salary."

***Kao v. Joy Holiday* (2017) 12 Cal.App.5th 947** applies Mamika to a payment made a few days late (passage read via search, not the whole opinion): "Here, Kao's daily rate of pay is his hourly rate of $28.85 multiplied by eight hours of work for a sum of $230.80. That figure multiplied by five days of nonpayment totals $1,154." So N days late means N × the daily rate, and the penalty stops on the day of payment ("until paid").

**DLSE** (FAQ_Paydays.htm):

> "An employee who is discharged must be paid all of his or her wages, including accrued vacation, immediately at the time of termination. Labor Code Sections 201 and 227.3"

> "The waiting time penalty is an amount equal to the employee's daily rate of pay for each day the wages remain unpaid, up to a maximum of thirty (30) calendar days. Mamika v. Barca (1998) 68 Cal.App4th 487"

The DLSE also says the undisputed portion must be paid even where part of the amount is disputed: "If the employer fails to pay what is undisputed, the 'good faith' defense will be defeated whatever the outcome of the disputed wages. Labor Code Section 206". On earned commissions at discharge, the employer "must complete the necessary calculations and pay the commissions on the date of the termination in the case of a discharge."

**Overtime and commissions in the daily rate: open on the sources read.** Mamika and Kao both compute a straight-time daily rate (hourly × 8). Neither addresses whether regular overtime or commissions raise the "same rate." No primary authority read decides it. The companion file reports secondary authority (an unpublished *Mills* decision) reading "rate" more broadly. Treat the inclusion of overtime or commissions as unsettled, and use base daily wage as the conservative key.

## 2. California: vacation at separation

**Answer.** Vested vacation is wages, earned pro rata as labor is performed, and payable at the final rate on any separation. A policy may not forfeit it, so "use it or lose it" is unlawful, but a reasonable accrual cap is lawful (§227.3; Suastez; DLSE). The DLSE treats a combined PTO bank entirely as vacation. Separately designated paid sick leave need not be paid out (§246(g)(1)). A collective-bargaining agreement displaces §227.3 only by a "clear and unmistakable" waiver that mentions the statutory right or the statute (Choate).

**§227.3**, full text (as amended by Stats. 1976, ch. 1041):

> "Unless otherwise provided by a collective-bargaining agreement, whenever a contract of employment or employer policy provides for paid vacations, and an employee is terminated without having taken off his vested vacation time, all vested vacation shall be paid to him as wages at his final rate in accordance with such contract of employment or employer policy respecting eligibility or time served; provided, however, that an employment contract or employer policy shall not provide for forfeiture of vested vacation time upon termination. The Labor Commissioner or a designated representative, in the resolution of any dispute with regard to vested vacation time, shall apply the principles of equity and fairness."

***Suastez v. Plastic Dress-Up Co.* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 774, 784**, the holding:

> "The right to a paid vacation, when offered in an employer's policy or contract of employment, constitutes deferred wages for services rendered. Case law from this state and others, as well as principles of equity and justice, compel the conclusion that a proportionate right to a paid vacation 'vests' as the labor is rendered. Once vested, the right is protected from forfeiture, by section 227.3. On termination of employment, therefore, the statute requires that an employee be paid in wages for a pro rata share of his vacation pay."

At 783, the employer's policy controls the *amount*, not the timing of vesting: "the passage only means that the *amount* of vacation pay an employee is entitled to be paid as wages is to be determined with reference to the employer's policy."

**DLSE, Vacation FAQ**:

- Vesting and caps: "vacation time is earned, or vests, as labor is performed. … Vacation pay accrues (adds up) as it is earned, and cannot be forfeited, even upon termination of employment, regardless of the reason for the termination. (Suastez v. Plastic Dress Up (1982) 31 C3d 774) An employer can place a reasonable cap on vacation benefits that prevents an employee from earning vacation over a certain amount of hours. (Boothby v. Atlas Mechanical (1992) 6 Cal.App.4th 1595)" *(Boothby itself not read.)*
- Use-it-or-lose-it (Q4): "a policy that provides for the forfeiture of vacation pay that is not used by a specified date ('use it or lose it') is an illegal policy under California law and will not be recognized by the Labor Commissioner."
- Cap (Q5): "Unlike 'use it or lose it' policies, a vacation policy that places a 'cap' or 'ceiling' on vacation pay accruals is permissible. … If implementation of a 'cap' is a subterfuge to deny employees vacation or vacation benefits, the policy will not be recognized by the Labor Commissioner."
- Proration at separation (Q2): the payout "must be prorated on a daily basis and must be paid at the final rate of pay in effect as of the date of the separation." Worked example: 120 hrs/yr entitlement, quit on day 219 → 219/365 = 60% → 72 hrs × $13.00 = $936.00.
- Combined PTO bank (Q8): "DLSE takes the position that such a program is subject to the same rules as other vacation policies. Thus, for example, the 'paid time off' is earned on a day-by-day basis, vested paid time off days cannot be forfeited, the number of earned and accrued paid time off days can be capped, and if an employee has earned and accrued paid time off days that have not been used at the time the employment relationship ends, the employee must be paid for these days."
- Penalty (Q11): unpaid vacation at separation supports "a claim for the waiting time penalty pursuant to Labor Code Section 203."

**Paid sick leave: §246(g)(1)** (as amended by SB 616, eff. Jan. 1, 2024):

> "(1) Except as specified in paragraph (2), an employer is not required to provide compensation to an employee for accrued, unused paid sick days upon termination, resignation, retirement, or other separation from employment."

Paragraph (2) reinstates unused sick days on rehire within one year. §246(f) lets an employer meet the sick-leave mandate through "a paid leave policy or paid time off policy." Under the DLSE's PTO position, a bank that is general PTO rather than a designated sick bank is payable at separation as vacation.

**What "otherwise provided by a collective-bargaining agreement" requires: *Choate v. Celite Corp.* (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 1460.** The court adopts a clear-and-unmistakable waiver standard (at 1467):

> "To be clear and unmistakable, a waiver must do more than speak in '[b]road, general language.' … It must be specific, and mention either the statutory protection being waived or, at a minimum, the statute itself."

> "The Agreements here neither mention pro rata vacation pay nor cite section 227.3. Celite points out that the Agreements 'affirmatively address' vacation payments upon termination. But discussing a topic while at the same time saying nothing about the statutory right at issue does not effect a clear and unmistakable waiver of that right."

The court rejected the argument that "otherwise provide[]" allows an implied waiver: "By their very nature, waivers may be express or implied. In determining which type of waiver the Legislature intended to codify in section 227.3, we are not rewriting the statute; we are construing it."

Choate then **reversed the §203 penalty**. The employer was not willful, because "this is the first case to define the standard for waiver under section 227.3" (at 1468). The disposition orders summary adjudication *for Celite* on the waiting-time-penalties count. The case is also held not preempted by LMRA §301: checking whether a CBA "affirmatively mention[s] section 227.3 or its protections does not entail interpretation."

## 3. California: exempt status, minimum wages, overtime, §514

**Answer.** The 2026 state minimum wage is $16.90/hour (eff. Jan. 1, 2026). The exempt salary floor is 2 × $16.90 × 40 × 52 = **$70,304/year** ($5,858.67/month). The duties test requires that the employee be "primarily engaged" in exempt duties, meaning more than half of worktime. The salary test is keyed by statute and wage-order text to the *state* minimum wage, so local minimum wages don't raise it. Los Angeles City's minimum wage is $18.42 from July 1, 2026 ($17.87 before). San Francisco's is $19.61 from July 1, 2026 ($19.18 before). No further change is scheduled before Dec. 31, 2026. Daily overtime runs over 8 hours/day at 1.5×, and over 12 hours/day at 2× (§510). §514 exempts CBA employees from §§510–511 if the CBA provides premium overtime rates and a regular rate at least 30% above the state minimum wage.

**State minimum wage.** The DLSE Minimum Wage FAQ: "Effective January 1, 2026, the minimum wage is $16.90 per hour for all employers, not otherwise covered by a higher minimum wage specific to an industry or a locality." The FAQ table lists $16.50 for Jan. 1, 2025 and $16.90 for Jan. 1, 2026, for employers of every size.

**Exempt salary: DIR News Release 2025-118** (Dec. 5, 2025):

> "Minimum Salary Requirement for Exempt Employees 2026
> $16.90 × 2 × 40 hours/week × 52 weeks/year = $70,304
> To meet this initial requirement of the exemption test, an employee must earn no less than twice the state minimum wage for full-time employment.
> As of January 1, 2026, employees in California must earn an annual salary of at least $70,304 to meet this threshold."

**§515(a), (c), (e)**:

> "(a) The Industrial Welfare Commission may establish exemptions from the requirement that an overtime rate of compensation be paid pursuant to Sections 510 and 511 for executive, administrative, and professional employees, if the employee is primarily engaged in the duties that meet the test of the exemption, customarily and regularly exercises discretion and independent judgment in performing those duties, and earns a monthly salary equivalent to no less than two times the state minimum wage for full-time employment."
>
> "(c) For the purposes of subdivision (a), 'full-time employment' means employment in which an employee is employed for 40 hours per week."
>
> "(e) For the purposes of this section, 'primarily' means more than one-half of the employee's worktime."

**Wage Order 4, §1(A)(1)(e)–(f)**, executive exemption (the administrative exemption at (2)(f)–(g) and the professional at (3)(d) use the same salary words):

> "(e) Who is primarily engaged in duties which meet the test of the exemption. … The work actually performed by the employee during the course of the workweek must, first and foremost, be examined and the amount of time the employee spends on such work, together with the employer's realistic expectations and the realistic requirements of the job, shall be considered in determining whether the employee satisfies this requirement.
> (f) Such an employee must also earn a monthly salary equivalent to no less than two (2) times the state minimum wage for full-time employment. Full-time employment is defined in Labor Code Section 515(c) as 40 hours per week."

**Local minimum wage and the exemption.** §515(a) and every wage-order salary clause say "state minimum wage." DIR's release computes the threshold from $16.90 alone. The DLSE treats local rates as a separate, parallel floor ("if a local entity (city or county) has adopted a higher minimum wage, employees must be paid the local wage where it is higher than the state or federal minimum wage rates"). The Los Angeles memo defines coverage by reference to the state law: employees who "qualify as an Employee entitled to payment of a minimum wage from any Employer under the California minimum wage law, as provided under Section 1197 of the California Labor Code and wage orders." So an exempt employee is outside the LA ordinance's wage floor as well. No DLSE opinion letter on this point was found or read. The answer rests on the statutory text.

**Los Angeles City**: OWS memo, Jan. 30, 2026:

> "The minimum wage rate, effective July 1, 2026, will increase by $0.55 for a new minimum wage rate of $18.42 per hour."

The rate in force Jan. 1–June 30, 2026 is therefore $17.87 ($18.42 − $0.55, derived from the memo). Increases take effect each July 1 (LAMC §187.02(d)), so none is scheduled before Dec. 31, 2026.

**San Francisco**: OLSE page: "must be paid no less than the San Francisco minimum wage, currently $19.61 as of July 1, 2026." The OLSE historical rates PDF lists "July 1, 2026: $19.61 per hour" and "July 1, 2025: $19.18 per hour." The rate adjusts each July 1, so none is scheduled before Dec. 31, 2026.

**Daily overtime: §510(a)**:

> "Eight hours of labor constitutes a day's work. Any work in excess of eight hours in one workday and any work in excess of 40 hours in any one workweek and the first eight hours worked on the seventh day of work in any one workweek shall be compensated at the rate of no less than one and one-half times the regular rate of pay for an employee. Any work in excess of 12 hours in one day shall be compensated at the rate of no less than twice the regular rate of pay for an employee. In addition, any work in excess of eight hours on any seventh day of a workweek shall be compensated at the rate of no less than twice the regular rate of pay of an employee."

**§514**, the CBA exemption, in full:

> "Sections 510 and 511 do not apply to an employee covered by a valid collective bargaining agreement if the agreement expressly provides for the wages, hours of work, and working conditions of the employees, and if the agreement provides premium wage rates for all overtime hours worked and a regular hourly rate of pay for those employees of not less than 30 percent more than the state minimum wage."

Wage Order 4 §3(I) repeats the same test. At $16.90, 130% of the state minimum is **$21.97/hour** (computed). §514 is also keyed to the *state* minimum wage, not a local one.

## 4. New York: vacation at separation and 2026 exempt thresholds

**Answer.** Vacation pay is a "benefit or wage supplement" under §198-c and counts as "wages" under §190(1), but payout at separation follows the employer's own agreement or policy. A forfeiture provision is enforceable when employees had notice of it (Glenville Gage, which *upheld* a posted forfeiture rule). §195(5) requires notice of the vacation policy "in writing or by publicly posting." The bright-line rule that forfeiture requires *written* notice is NYSDOL enforcement guidance, read only secondhand; the court text read does not state it. The 2026 executive and administrative salary floor is **$1,275.00/week** in NYC, Long Island and Westchester and **$1,199.10/week** in the rest of the state. NY sets no salary floor for professionals.

**§198-c** (nysenate.gov, revision of 2024-03-15):

> "2. As used in this section, the term 'benefits or wage supplements' includes, but is not limited to, reimbursement for expenses; health, welfare and retirement benefits; and vacation, separation or holiday pay.
> 3. This section shall not apply to any person in a bona fide executive, administrative, or professional capacity whose earnings are in excess of one thousand three hundred dollars a week."

§198-c(1) makes failing to pay agreed benefits "within thirty days after such payments are required to be made" a misdemeanor.

**§190(1)** (revision of 2024-10-04):

> "The term 'wages' also includes benefits or wage supplements as defined in section one hundred ninety-eight-c of this article, except for the purposes of sections one hundred ninety-one and one hundred ninety-two of this article."

**§191(3)**, final pay timing (revision of 2014-09-22): "If employment is terminated, the employer shall pay the wages not later than the regular pay day for the pay period during which the termination occurred". Because of §190(1), this timing rule does not reach vacation pay (Glenville Gage, 70 A.D.2d at 286).

**§195(5)** (revision of 2020-10-02):

> "5. notify his employees in writing or by publicly posting the employer's policy on sick leave, vacation, personal leave, holidays and hours."

***Glenville Gage Co. v. Industrial Bd. of Appeals*, 70 A.D.2d 283 (3d Dep't 1979), aff'd for the reasons stated, 52 N.Y.2d 777 (1980).** The employer's posted notice said "ALL EMPLOYEES WHO ARE TERMINATED OR QUIT BEFORE VACATION PERIODS FORFEIT THEIR VACATION PAY" (at 285). The court **annulled** the Board's order requiring payment. At 286:

> "All that is required by section 198-c is that an employer abide by the terms of his agreement to provide benefits. Further, there is no substantial evidence in this record which could support a finding that the employees were not aware of the vacation pay policy of the employer regardless of their precise knowledge of an agreement at the time they commenced their employment."

The court distinguished *De Vita v Rand McNally* (44 Misc 2d 906), where the "plaintiff did not have knowledge or notice of these rules since they were not told to him nor were they posted in a convenient place or published so as to place him on notice of them." So notice is what makes the forfeiture bind. In Glenville Gage, notice was shown by a posted written notice plus the employees' "general knowledge of the practice."

Later federal courts read the line the same way. *Kolesnikow v. Hudson Valley Hosp. Ctr.*, 622 F. Supp. 2d 98, 120 (S.D.N.Y. 2009): "An employee's entitlement to receive payment for accrued, unused paid time off upon termination of employment is governed by the terms of the employer's publicized policy." (Passage read via search, as quoted in *Sosnowy v. A. Perri Farms*, 764 F. Supp. 2d 457 (E.D.N.Y. 2011).)

**NYSDOL's "written forfeiture notice" rule: [secondary]; the NYSDOL text is UNREAD.** Secondary sources (LawHelpNY and payroll guides) say the NYSDOL requires payout of accrued vacation unless the employer's written policy gave notice of the forfeiture. The NYSDOL page stating this could not be reached (dol.ny.gov search renders client-side). Key it as agency enforcement policy, not as the holding of Glenville Gage.

**2026 exempt thresholds.** 12 NYCRR 142-2.14 itself is **UNREAD**. The NYSDOL's own Part 142 Wage Order Summary (rev. 12/25) gives, under "EXECUTIVE AND ADMINISTRATIVE EXEMPTION (per week) … MINIMUM SALARY REQUIRED":

| Region | as of 01/01/25 | as of 01/01/26 |
|---|---|---|
| NYC, large employers (11 or more) | $1,237.50 | $1,275.00 |
| NYC, small employers (10 or fewer) | $1,237.50 | $1,275.00 |
| Long Island & Westchester | $1,237.50 | $1,275.00 |
| Remainder of New York State | $1,161.65 | $1,199.10 |

The same summary lists the 2026 basic minimum wage at $17.00 (NYC, Long Island, Westchester) and $16.00 (rest of state). The downstate threshold equals 75 × $17.00 (computed). The NYSDOL minimum wage page says that on January 1, 2027 the minimum wage "will stay the same," because an inflation "off-ramp" was triggered. No change falls before Dec. 31, 2026. The professional exemption has no NY salary floor in the summary.

## 5. Texas

**Answer.** Accrued vacation is "wages" under the Payday Law only if it is owed "under a written agreement with the employer or under a written policy of the employer" (§61.001(7)(B)). With no such writing, nothing is owed, and the writing's terms control. A discharged employee must be paid in full "not later than the sixth day after the date the employee is discharged" (§61.014(a)), and "day" means a calendar day (§61.001(2)).

**§61.001** (Tex. Labor Code):

> "(2) 'Day' means a calendar day."
>
> "(7) 'Wages' means compensation owed by an employer for:
> (A) labor or services rendered by an employee, whether computed on a time, task, piece, commission, or other basis; and
> (B) vacation pay, holiday pay, sick leave pay, parental leave pay, or severance pay owed to an employee under a written agreement with the employer or under a written policy of the employer."

**§61.014**:

> "(a) An employer shall pay in full an employee who is discharged from employment not later than the sixth day after the date the employee is discharged.
> (b) An employer shall pay in full an employee who leaves employment other than by discharge not later than the next regularly scheduled payday."

**§61.015(a)**: commissions and bonuses "are due according to the terms of: (1) an agreement between the employee and employer; or (2) an applicable collective bargaining agreement."

**TWC guidance** (*Texas Guide for Employers*):

- Accrued Leave Payouts: "Payouts of accrued leave are required under the Texas Payday Law only if such a payment is promised by the employer in a written policy or agreement. The payout would be controlled by the wording of the policy or agreement. If no such policy exists, the company would not owe such a payment." TWC's sample policy conditions payout on an economic layoff or two weeks' written notice. TWC says the policy can lawfully deny payout to an employee "terminated for any reason other than an economic layoff."
- Final Pay: "If an employee is laid off, discharged, fired, or otherwise involuntarily separated from employment, the final pay is due within six (6) calendar days of discharge." Fringe benefits and commissions share that deadline "unless a different payout schedule is provided in the wage agreement or policy relating to that particular component of the pay."

## 6. Federal FLSA salary level for 2026

**Answer.** The standard salary level is **$684/week ($35,568/year)**, and the highly compensated employee total is $107,432/year. Both are the 2019-rule figures, in force because the 2024 rule was vacated on November 15, 2024 (*Texas v. U.S. Dep't of Labor*, 756 F. Supp. 3d 361 (E.D. Tex. 2024)). DOL removed the vacated text from the CFR on May 15, 2026 (91 FR 27833). No proposed rule on Part 541 appears in the Federal Register for 2025–2026, so no change is scheduled before Dec. 31, 2026.

**DOL WHD earnings-thresholds page**: "Standard Salary Level $684 per week (equivalent to a $35,568 annual salary)"; "Total Annual Compensation Requirement for Highly Compensated Employees $107,432 per year, including at least $684 per week paid on a salary or fee basis." The page adds: "These earnings thresholds do not apply to certain types of employees, including doctors, lawyers, teachers, and outside sales employees."

**91 FR 27833 (May 15, 2026), FR Doc. 2026-09839**, effective May 15, 2026, "Implementation of Federal Court Judgments":

> "On November 15, 2024, the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Texas issued an order vacating the 2024 rule in Texas v. U.S. Dep't of Labor, 756 F. Supp. 3d 361 (E.D. Tex. 2024). On December 30, 2024, the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Texas likewise issued an order vacating the 2024 rule in Flint Avenue, LLC v. U.S. Dep't of Labor, No. 5:24-cv-00130-C, Dkt. No. 62. These orders remain final judgments following the dismissal of the appeal in each case by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit on May 5 and 7, 2026, respectively."

> "In light of these judgments, the operative version of the Department's part 541 regulations is the version of these regulations that was in place on June 30, 2024, prior to the effective date of the 2024 rule, and which the Department has been enforcing."

The vacated 2024 rule would have raised the level to $844/week on July 1, 2024 and $1,128/week on January 1, 2025, with triennial updates. Footnote 14 says the technical amendment "does not foreclose the Department from engaging in notice and comment rulemaking in the future." The restored § 541.100(a)(1) reads "at a rate of not less than $684 per week."

A Federal Register API query (CFR 29 Part 541, published on or after 2025-01-01) returns only this one document. No NPRM was published.
